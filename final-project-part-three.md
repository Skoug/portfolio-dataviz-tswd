# Changes made since Part II

Since completing Part II, I received additional feedback from my professor. I focused on adding narrative elements to my story: extra standout facts, callouts to my audience, and refining my call to action. First, I addressed my audience immediately after my personal story introduction. I added extra information on how important farmers are to the Great Lakes Region and their contribution to American and Canadian food supplies. Then, I ensured that my introduction on the science about algae did not patronize the farmers, either. To finish my changes to the introduction, I wanted to highlight the dangers of hypoxia. I incldued the minimum value of dissolved oxygen in Lake Erie recorded this summer, which happened to be under the threshold of "stressing" fish, and just above immediate danger. I believe this callout adds tangible danger to farmer's concerns about algae.

I made additional changes to my section on the economic impact of algae blooms. Previously, the connection between tourism and economic impact was a bit more abstract. The data visualization showed the monetary impacts of tourism on the economy, but not the human impacts. So, I included a rough estimate of the number of people who visit the Great Lakes yearly. This value, around 250 million people, represents a huge portion of the entire U.S. and brings the scale of tourism into focus. Farmers can acknowledge who their actions harm and not just how their actions harm society.

Then, I updated my call to action: previously, my call to action was more abstract, so I gave concrete advice on how to limit the spread of algae blooms. The original call to action read along the lines of, "farmers must adapt ". It now reads, "The farming community needs to adopt new technologies to protect our environment." This change centers technology change and adaptation as a necessity for improvement. 

## The audience

I identified my final audience as farmers around the Great Lakes Region. Farmers, no matter where they are from along the coastline, contribute to fertilizer runoff into the Great Lakes. I changed the framing of my project to Canadian and American farmers because both create algae blooms. Canada should be a relatively equal stakeholder to the U.S., seeing as 4 of the 5 Great Lakes have Canadian coastline. Though my monetary analysis used USD, I wanted the rest of my analysis and recommendations to apply to both countries. However, I am not sure if Canada has placed more limits on the use of fertilizer than the United States. Their farming practices might lessen the impact of runoff, but algae still form along the Northern borders of the Great Lakes from their runoff. 

I believe the majority of my data focused interest toward Western Lake Erie. The farmers in this area might be blamed more for ecosystem collapse due to my analysis-- this was not my goal. Runoff from different areas along Lake Erie aggregates in this zone due to its position relative to lake currents. Ecosystem collapse cannot be attributed to runoff from one region or country in particular along the lake. All farmers who contribute runoff are complicit. 


## Final design decisions

My design remained relatively similar to the storyboard I designed and adjusted in Part II. The storyboard itself used green as an alert color, which I incorporated into my data visualizations. In Tableau, I used either the green shade gradient or the green-yellow gradient to highlight discrepancies. I changed the colors of the two map visualizations, but their content remained effectively the same between Parts II and III. Alternatively, my economic bar chart visualization did change. Originally, I included all economic sectors defined by NOAA in this viz: some only gained approximately 200,000 in profit annually. To increase ease of understanding for my viewers, I removed the relatively inconsequential economic sectors and instead chose to focus on shipping and tourism. These are the two largest lake-related industries by far. They alos highlight an important distinction-- shipping will not be affected by algae blooms, but tourism will. Tourism, the larger economic force, needs to be protected via limiting the growth of harmful algae blooms (HABs).
